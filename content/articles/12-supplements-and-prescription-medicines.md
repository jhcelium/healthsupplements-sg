## Why a supplement and a medicine can interact

"Natural" does not mean "no effect." Singapore's HealthHub, the national health portal, puts it directly: natural ingredients do not mean no-risk, and "overdosing, combining supplements or taking them with some medicines may cause harm" ([HealthHub, *Understanding Health Supplements*](https://www.healthhub.sg/live-healthy/understandinghealthsupplements_hsa)).

Interactions generally fall into a few patterns. A supplement and a medicine can act on the same system and add up — for example, ingredients with mild blood-thinning effects layered on top of a prescription blood thinner. A supplement can change how quickly the body processes a drug, raising or lowering the drug's level. Or the timing of two products taken together can affect how much of either is absorbed. The size of any real-world effect varies a lot between people and products, which is exactly why a personalised check beats a blanket rule.

For the broader mindset behind this — moderation, overlap awareness, and avoiding hype — see our companion note on [staying safe without the hype](/articles/09-safety-no-hype).

## Common questions Singaporeans ask

One question comes up again and again in local discussion: *"I'm on a blood thinner — can I still take fish oil or turmeric?"* It is a fair question, because both are popular here and both have mild blood-related effects.

The honest answer is "it depends, and it's worth checking." Drug-information references note that omega-3 (fish oil) may, in some cases, add to the bleeding risk of medicines such as warfarin, while other studies in monitored patients found no significant change to warfarin control — a genuinely mixed evidence picture ([Drugs.com interaction reference](https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/fish-oil-with-warfarin-1749-3873-2311-0.html)). Turmeric is itself a mild blood thinner, so combining it with anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicines is commonly flagged as something to clear with a clinician first ([Drugs.com turmeric reference](https://www.drugs.com/drug-interactions/fish-oil-with-turmeric-1749-3873-2682-0.html)).

Because the evidence is mixed rather than absolute, it helps to know how to weigh a supplement claim in the first place — our guide on [supplement claims and evidence](/articles/11-evidence-based-claims) covers that. The practical takeaway is not a yes/no rule but a habit: tell your doctor or pharmacist *everything* you take. HealthHub also notes that anyone scheduled for surgery should inform their doctor about supplements, since some may need to be paused beforehand. None of this means a supplement is "bad" — it means the combination deserves a quick professional check rather than a guess.

## What HSA's safety alerts actually show

Singapore's Health Sciences Authority (HSA) regularly publishes consumer alerts, and the pattern in them is informative. In March 2025, HSA warned the public against four products sold online that were found to contain potent or banned ingredients — including high levels of arsenic and the banned weight-loss substance sibutramine — after two consumers suffered adverse effects ([HSA alert, March 2025](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/announcements/hsa-alert-four-adulterated-products/)). In its 2025 enforcement reporting, HSA noted more than 1.06 million units of illegal health products worth over S$750,000 seized, with cough syrups (about 56%), sexual-enhancement products (about 19%), and sedatives and painkillers (about 17%) making up most of the haul.

The lesson for the supplement–medicine question is twofold. First, a product marketed as a "supplement" can secretly contain an actual pharmaceutical ingredient — which means a hidden interaction you never agreed to. Second, the products most often adulterated are exactly the ones promising fast, dramatic results. The most up-to-date list of these warnings lives on HSA's own [consumer safety pages](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/consumer-safety). For the standard signals to read on a legitimate label, see our [label checklist](/label-checklist).

## Why "HSA-notified" is not "interaction-checked"

A common misunderstanding in Singapore is that health supplements are individually approved by HSA. They are not. HSA's own regulatory overview states that health supplements are not subject to approval and licensing by HSA for import, manufacture, or sale; instead, dealers are responsible for ensuring their products meet safety and quality standards, and notification is voluntary ([HSA, *Regulatory overview of health supplements*](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/health-supplements/overview/)).

That matters here: even a perfectly legitimate, well-made supplement has not been individually assessed for how it interacts with *your* specific prescription. Regulation sets a safety-and-quality floor for the product; it does not check the combination on your shelf. That gap is what a pharmacist fills. ## What to ask your pharmacist before combining

You don't need clinical knowledge to have a useful conversation. Bring the actual boxes or a photo of the labels to any community pharmacy — Guardian, Watsons, or Unity counters all have a pharmacist on hand — and ask plainly: *"I take this medicine; is it okay to add this supplement, and does timing matter?"* If you use more than one pharmacy or clinic, make sure each one knows your full list, since no single record may capture everything.

A few simple habits reduce risk more than any single rule: keep a short written list of everything you take, change one thing at a time so any effect is easier to trace, and raise supplements before scheduled surgery or a new prescription. If you ever feel unwell after starting a supplement, HealthHub's guidance is to see your doctor or pharmacist promptly. More everyday questions are answered on our [FAQ](/faq).

## The bottom line

Most people combine supplements and medicines without incident — but "most" is not "all," and the cases that go wrong are usually preventable with one short conversation. Treat any overlap between a supplement and a prescription as a question for your pharmacist, not a guess for the internet. That single habit is the most evidence-aware thing you can do.
