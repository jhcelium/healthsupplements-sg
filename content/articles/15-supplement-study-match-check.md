*Written by the Health Supplements Singapore editorial team · Last updated: 25 July 2026*

A supplement study match check asks whether the research being cited actually resembles the product claim in front of you. This Singapore guide is general education, not medical advice: it offers a practical way to compare the ingredient, amount, participants, study outcome, and claim before treating a research reference as relevant.

## What a study match check can show

[HSA's January 2025 claims-substantiation guidance](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-chp-claims-guidelines.pdf) says health-supplement claims must be supported by evidence relevant to the claim. Evidence may concern the finished product or, when that is unavailable, its ingredients; HSA may ask a dealer to justify how ingredient evidence relates to the product claim. The January 2026 [Health Supplements Guidelines](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-hs-guidelines.pdf) repeat that claim evidence should be good quality and relevant.

That creates a useful consumer question: does the cited study match what the label or advertisement is saying? A match does not prove that a supplement is appropriate for you, and a mismatch does not by itself prove that a product is non-compliant. It simply identifies where the evidence link is clear, uncertain, or missing.

## Start with the exact product claim

Write down the complete claim rather than a shortened slogan. HSA describes a health claim as having an action and a target. For example, "supports normal immune function" contains the action "supports" and the target "normal immune function." The evidence should address that combination, not merely mention the same ingredient.

Next, record whether the research studied the finished product or only one ingredient. Check the ingredient name or form, the amount used, and the recommended daily dose on the current label. Do not assume that two products are interchangeable because their front labels use the same familiar ingredient name.

## Use a five-field match record

The table below is an original reading record derived from [HSA's relevance rule](https://file.go.gov.sg/hsa-chp-claims-guidelines.pdf), [WHO trial guidance](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/clinical-trials), and [NCCIH research-literacy guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/providers/digest/know-the-science-of-complementary-health-approaches-science). It was assembled and source-checked on 25 July 2026.

| Field | Compare the label or claim with the study | Caution signal |
|---|---|---|
| Product or ingredient | Finished formula, ingredient name, and stated form | The study used a different formula or the form is not disclosed |
| Amount and schedule | Recommended daily amount versus the studied intervention | The advertisement gives no way to compare amounts |
| Participants | Age range and relevant characteristics | Results from a narrow group are presented as universal |
| Duration | Study period versus the implied time frame of the claim | A short study is used to suggest an indefinite result |
| Outcome | What the study measured versus the claim's action and target | A laboratory marker is presented as a broader health outcome |

Treat an unclear field as a question, not as permission to fill the gap yourself. A study abstract, retailer summary, or influencer post may omit details needed for a fair comparison.

## Separate one study from the wider evidence

HSA's guidance accepts well-designed observational or interventional human studies when suitable published references are unavailable. It does not treat open internet sources, product websites, personal testimonials, or solely animal or in-vitro studies as acceptable claim-substantiation evidence.

Study type still does not settle the question on its own. WHO defines a clinical trial as prospectively assigning people to an intervention to evaluate effects on health outcomes. WHO also explains that trial registration helps people identify publication bias and selective reporting. A registration record can therefore add transparency, but it is not the same as a completed result.

NCCIH advises checking study size and date and looking for results repeated by different investigators. Its guidance notes that a single study rarely provides a final answer. For a buyer, the practical lesson is to read one promising result as one part of a larger evidence record.

## A seven-step study match checklist

This original checklist was assembled from the same verified HSA, WHO, and NCCIH sources on 25 July 2026.

1. Copy the exact claim, including qualifiers such as "may" or "healthy adults."
2. Record whether the citation concerns the finished product or an ingredient.
3. Match the ingredient name, form, amount, and schedule where those details are available.
4. Compare the study participants and duration with the people and time frame implied by the claim.
5. Check whether the measured outcome is the same as the claim's action and target.
6. Look for a trial registration and for other studies by independent investigators.
7. Mark every missing detail as unknown and ask the seller, pharmacist, or doctor before making a personal health decision.

This checklist is an educational comparison tool, not a score of product quality or a substitute for professional review.

## Questions people ask

**Does a human study automatically prove a supplement claim?** No. Human research is more directly relevant than animal-only or in-vitro evidence, but the product, amount, participants, duration, outcome, study quality, and wider evidence still matter.

**Is a clinical-trial registration the same as a published result?** No. A registry records planned trial information and improves transparency. It does not show by itself that the trial finished or that the result supports a claim.

**Can an ingredient study support a finished-product claim?** HSA allows ingredient evidence when finished-product evidence is unavailable, but the relationship to the claim may need justification. A consumer should check how closely the ingredient, amount, and intended claim match.

**What if the study details are not available?** Treat the evidence as incomplete. Do not infer missing amounts, participant details, or outcomes from a headline or testimonial.
