*Written by the Health Supplements Singapore editorial team · Last updated: 30 June 2026*

An NRV label check helps Singapore supplement buyers understand whether a vitamin or mineral claim is tied to a meaningful daily amount. This guide is general education, not medical advice: it explains how HSA claim rules, Singapore dietary reference points, and label wording fit together before you compare products or ask a pharmacist for personal guidance.

## What NRV means on a supplement label

NRV means nutrient reference value. In supplement shopping, it is a reference point for comparing the amount of a vitamin or mineral in one serving against a daily benchmark. It is not a personal target, a treatment plan, or proof that a product is useful for you.

For Singapore readers, the safer way to use NRV is as a label-reading filter. If a product makes a vitamin or mineral claim, ask three questions: what nutrient is named, how much is supplied per recommended daily dose, and whether the wording stays within health-maintenance language rather than disease language.

## The Singapore claim threshold to know

HSA's health supplement claims guidance says vitamin and mineral health claims need enough of the nutrient per recommended daily dose. The guidance points to at least 15% of the Codex nutrient reference value, or the local recommended dietary allowance, before that vitamin or mineral claim can be made.

That is a useful original check for buyers because it connects the claim to the serving instruction, not just to the bottle size. A product may look generous when the label lists a large container total, but HSA's logic is based on what the recommended daily dose provides.

| Label item to check | Why it matters | Source-backed note |
|---|---|---|
| Nutrient amount per serving | Shows what you actually take per dose | HSA focuses on the recommended daily dose |
| Percentage or reference value | Helps compare against an NRV or RDA benchmark | HSA uses a 15% threshold for vitamin/mineral claims |
| Claim wording | Separates support language from disease claims | HSA claim lists and guidance restrict disease-style claims |

## Do not treat the percentage as a dosing instruction

A higher percentage is not automatically better. HealthHub publishes Singapore recommended dietary allowances for vitamins and minerals, but those values are population reference points, not personal instructions. Age, pregnancy, diet, existing medicines, blood-test results, and medical conditions can change what is appropriate.

This is where an NRV check should stop: it can identify whether a label looks coherent, but it cannot tell you whether to start, stop, or raise a supplement dose. If you are using prescription medicines or managing a health condition, take the label to a pharmacist or doctor rather than making a private dose change.

## A simple buyer checklist

Use this quick sequence before you compare two vitamin or mineral products:

1. Find the recommended daily dose, not only the capsule or tablet count.
2. Write down the nutrient amount delivered by that daily dose.
3. Compare any percentage or claim against the named nutrient, not the whole product.
4. Watch for disease wording such as treat, prevent, cure, reverse, or clinically proven.
5. Add the same nutrient across every product you already take.
6. Ask a pharmacist if the label is unclear, the dose looks high, or medicines are involved.

## Claim wording that deserves caution

HSA's claims guidance separates health-maintenance claims from disease claims. For a label reader, that means "supports normal immune function" is a different kind of statement from "prevents flu" or "clinically proven to treat deficiency." The first still needs evidence and an appropriate nutrient amount; the second can move into disease or medical territory.

The most practical red flag is when a product uses a nutrient percentage to imply a medical outcome. An NRV or RDA reference can help explain nutrient amount, but it should not be used to promise diagnosis, treatment, cure, or guaranteed results.

## Questions people ask

**Does 100% NRV mean I need that supplement every day?** No. It only describes a label amount against a reference value. Your diet and personal health context still matter.

**Is a product weak if it has less than 100% NRV?** Not necessarily. Some products are designed to supplement diet rather than replace all intake. The key is whether the claim is proportionate and the label is clear.

**Can I use NRV to compare multivitamins?** Yes, as a first-pass label check. Compare the same nutrient per recommended daily dose, then check for overlap with other products.
