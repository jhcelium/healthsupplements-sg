*Written by the Health Supplements Singapore editorial team · Last updated: 30 July 2026*

Supplement research funding tells you who supported a study, but the funder's name is only the start of the check. This Singapore guide is general education, not medical advice: it shows how to read funding, sponsor-role, author-disclosure, and data-access statements before deciding how much weight to give research used around a supplement claim.

## Why the funding statement matters

[HSA's health-supplement claims guidance](https://www.hsa.gov.sg/health-supplements/claims/) says claims should have good-quality evidence relevant to the claim. It also says sellers should not misuse or exaggerate research results or use scientific data that the public cannot verify. The guidance does not say that the identity of a funder alone determines whether a study is good or bad.

Funding is therefore a transparency question, not a shortcut verdict. Public agencies, universities, charities, and companies can support research. A useful reading check asks who paid, what role that party had, whether author relationships were declared, and whether the study itself matches the product claim.

## Read three statements separately

A paper may place relevant information under different headings. The **funding statement** identifies direct support for the work. A **sponsor-role statement** may say whether the supporter influenced study design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing, or publication. A **conflict-of-interest or author-disclosure statement** covers relationships that may be relevant even when they did not directly fund that study.

The [International Committee of Medical Journal Editors](https://icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/author-responsibilities--conflicts-of-interest.html) recommends reporting sources of support, the sponsor's role, publication restrictions, author relationships, and the authors' access to study data. It also distinguishes direct study support from broader support for an author's time. That is why finding "no conflicts declared" does not answer every funding question, and finding company support does not by itself describe what the company did.

## Use a six-field disclosure record

This original record was assembled from the verified HSA, ICMJE, and [NCCIH research-literacy guidance](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/providers/digest/know-the-science-of-complementary-health-approaches-science) and source-checked on 30 July 2026.

| Field to record | What to look for | If it is missing |
|---|---|---|
| Direct funder | Named organisation or a statement that no specific funding supported the work | Mark the funding source unclear |
| Sponsor role | Design, data collection, analysis, interpretation, writing, or publication role | Do not assume either involvement or independence |
| Data access | Which authors could inspect the full study data | Mark data access unclear |
| Author relationships | Employment, consulting, shares, patents, fees, or other declared activities | Record only what the paper states |
| Product connection | Whether the funder or an author has an identifiable interest in the studied product | Treat the connection as context, not a verdict |
| Wider evidence | Similar findings from other investigators and current sources | Avoid treating one paper as the final answer |

Keep a link or DOI and the date you checked it. If the statement is absent, use "not found" rather than "none": those phrases do not mean the same thing.

## What disclosure can and cannot show

Disclosure can show who supported the work and which relationships or roles were reported. It can help a reader identify questions about access, analysis, writing, or publication. It cannot, by itself, prove that a result is correct, incorrect, independent, biased, safe, or suitable for a particular person.

NCCIH suggests asking who runs and pays for an information source, checking whether information is current, and looking for findings repeated by different investigators. It notes that a single study rarely gives a final answer. Apply those questions together with the study-match fields: product or ingredient, amount, participants, duration, and outcome.

A careful summary might say, "The paper reports company funding and states that the sponsor had no role in analysis." It should not jump from that sentence to "the supplement works" or "the research is invalid." Both conclusions need evidence the funding line alone cannot provide.

## A seven-step funding check

This original sequence was assembled from the same three verified sources on 30 July 2026.

1. Open the full paper or journal record rather than relying on an advertisement or screenshot.
2. Find the funding, acknowledgements, sponsor-role, and author-disclosure sections.
3. Write down the direct funder and any support provided to individual authors separately.
4. Record every stated sponsor role, including any restriction on publication.
5. Check who had access to the data and who takes responsibility for the analysis.
6. Run a separate study match check for the ingredient, amount, participants, duration, and outcome.
7. Look for current evidence from other investigators, then label every missing detail as unknown.

This checklist is a reading aid. It is not a compliance ruling, a research-quality score, or a recommendation to use or avoid a supplement.

## Questions people ask

**Does company funding mean a supplement study is unreliable?** Not automatically. Record the funder, sponsor role, author relationships, data access, study methods, and wider evidence. Funding is relevant context, not a standalone result.

**Is "no conflict of interest" the same as "no funding"?** No. A paper can report direct study funding separately from author relationships. Read the funding and disclosure sections rather than treating one line as both.

**What if the sponsor role is not stated?** Mark it as not found or unclear. Do not infer that the sponsor controlled the study, and do not infer that it had no involvement.

**Can a disclosure statement prove a supplement claim?** No. HSA's relevance and quality principles still apply, and the research must still match the product claim. A transparent disclosure helps interpretation but does not establish efficacy or personal suitability.
